How Pool Water Gets Cleaned — The Simple Flow

That's it. The pump is the engine that keeps water moving through this loop. The filter is what actually cleans the water. Everything else on the equipment pad either supports this loop, controls it, or monitors it. Once you understand the loop, every individual part makes sense.

1. Equipment Pad Components

The equipment pad is the area beside the pool where all the mechanical parts are located. You'll inspect this at every service stop. Most problems can be caught early if you know what to look and listen for.

🔄 Pump
The heart of the system. Without the pump, water sits still and gets dirty fast. It pulls water out of the pool through the skimmer and main drain, pushes it through the filter, and sends it back through the return jets. Listen for warning sounds: rattling (debris stuck in the impeller), grinding (bearings wearing out), or a sucking/air sound (air getting into the lines).
🗑️ Strainer Basket
The pump's bodyguard. It sits inside the pump housing and catches leaves, twigs, and bugs before they can get into the spinning impeller inside the pump. Think of it like the lint trap in a dryer — if it's full, nothing flows. Clean it at every visit. A clogged basket starves the pump of water, which causes it to overheat and wear out faster.
🔵 Filter
Removes fine particles from the water. Three types: sand, D.E., and cartridge. Each cleaned differently. Monitor the pressure gauge — rising pressure means the filter needs cleaning.
📊 Pressure Gauge
The filter's health meter. PSI (pounds per square inch) is the unit it measures in — just think of it as a number that goes up when the filter gets dirtier. A clean filter runs at a low, normal pressure (typically 10–15 PSI). As the filter collects more dirt, pressure builds. When pressure rises 8–10 PSI above that normal number, it's time to clean the filter. Always note the clean baseline at each visit.
🔥 Heater
Heats pool water for comfort. Gas or heat pump. Check for unusual noises, error codes on the display, and proper ignition. Low water flow through the heater can cause damage.
⏰ Timeclock
Controls when the pump runs. Verify the on/off pins are set correctly at every visit — an incorrect timeclock setting is a common cause of water chemistry failures.

2. The Three Filter Types

There are three types of pool filters. They all do the same job (clean the water), but they do it differently and they each need a different cleaning method. Knowing which type of filter you're dealing with at each pool is essential — because using the wrong cleaning method can damage the filter.

What is a micron? Filters are rated by how small a particle they can catch, measured in microns. One micron is 1/1000th of a millimeter — too small to see with the naked eye. A human hair is about 70 microns wide. So a filter rated at 2 microns catches things far smaller than anything you can see. The lower the micron number, the finer the filtration.

Filter Type Filtration Cleaning Method After Cleaning
Sand ~20 microns Backwash, then Rinse Return to Filter position
D.E. ~2 microns (finest) Backwash, then add fresh D.E. Add D.E. through skimmer
Cartridge ~10 microns Remove, rinse with hose Reinstall, no backwash
⛔ Never backwash a cartridge filter. There is no backwash valve on cartridge filters. Remove the element, rinse it from top to bottom between the pleats with a garden hose. Soak in filter cleaner solution monthly or quarterly.

3. When and How to Backwash

What is backwashing? Backwashing means running water backwards through the filter to flush out the dirt that's built up inside. Think of it like blowing backwards through a clogged straw to clear it. You do this for sand and D.E. filters — cartridge filters are cleaned a completely different way (just remove and rinse).

When to backwash: Check the pressure gauge. If the current reading is 8–10 PSI higher than the normal clean baseline, it's time to backwash. At that point, the filter is so packed with dirt that water can barely push through, and it's no longer cleaning effectively.

Backwash Process (Sand Filter) — Step by Step

  1. Turn pump OFF
  2. Turn multiport valve to BACKWASH
  3. Turn pump ON — run until waste water runs clear (30–90 seconds)
  4. Turn pump OFF again
  5. Turn valve to RINSE — run for 15–30 seconds
  6. Turn pump OFF
  7. Turn valve back to FILTER
  8. Turn pump ON — check new pressure reading (should be back to baseline)
Always run Rinse after backwashing. Skipping the Rinse step sends disturbed sand into the pool, causing cloudiness. 15–30 seconds is enough.
