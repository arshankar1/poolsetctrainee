Before We Start: Key Terms Explained

Pool care involves some science words that might be new to you. Before we dive in, here's a plain-English explanation of each one. You'll see these terms throughout this module and every module after it.

What is pH?

pH is just a number from 0 to 14. It tells you how acidic or "basic" the water is. A 7 is neutral — like pure water. Below 7 is acidic (like lemon juice). Above 7 is basic (like baking soda). Pool water should stay between 7.2 and 7.6 — slightly basic. That's the sweet spot where chlorine works best and water feels comfortable to swim in.

What is ppm?

ppm stands for "parts per million." It's a way to measure tiny amounts of something in water. Here's an easy way to picture it: imagine filling a million cups of water. If 1 of those cups had something added to it, that's 1 ppm. Pool chemicals are measured in ppm because even small amounts make a big difference.

What does chlorine do?

Chlorine is a disinfectant. That means it kills germs, bacteria, and algae in the water. Without chlorine, a pool would quickly become unsafe to swim in. Think of chlorine as the pool's immune system — it's constantly fighting off anything that could make swimmers sick.

What is alkalinity?

Alkalinity is a measure of the water's ability to resist changes in pH. Think of it like a shock absorber. When alkalinity is at the right level, your pH stays stable — it doesn't jump around when you add chemicals. When alkalinity is low, pH bounces around wildly and becomes very hard to control. We'll cover the right range shortly.

1. The Three Elements of Pool Care

A healthy pool needs three things working together at all times. If any one of them is missing or broken, the other two can't make up for it. Think of it like a three-legged stool — all three legs have to be there or the whole thing falls over.

⚗️ Chemistry

Chemistry is about keeping the water balanced. That means the right amount of chlorine to kill germs, the right pH so the water doesn't irritate skin or eyes, and several other measurements that we'll cover in this module.

When chemistry is off, you get cloudy water, algae, or water that damages the pool itself. pH is the most important number to get right — it controls whether your chlorine can actually do its job. At the right pH (7.2–7.6), about half your chlorine is active and working. At a high pH of 8.0, only about 20% is working. So even if the chlorine level looks fine on a test, bad pH can make it almost useless.

🔄 Circulation

Circulation means the water is constantly moving. The pump pulls water out of the pool, pushes it through the filter to clean it, then sends it back. Without that movement, chemicals can't spread evenly, and algae starts growing in the still corners.

The pump needs to run 8–12 hours per day in summer. There's a timer (called a timeclock) that controls this. Part of your job is checking that it's set correctly at every visit.

🧹 Maintenance

Maintenance is the physical cleaning — skimming leaves off the surface, brushing the walls and floor, and vacuuming up debris that settles on the bottom. Chemicals alone can't do this job.

Here's why brushing matters even when the pool looks clean: algae starts as a thin, invisible film on the pool walls before it turns green. Regular brushing breaks that film and stops algae before it ever becomes a problem. An ounce of brushing is worth a pound of algaecide.

2. Key Chemistry Ranges

At every service stop, you'll test the pool water and check five numbers. Each number tells you something different about the water's health. The goal is to keep each number inside its "ideal range." Too low or too high — and something goes wrong.

Here's a quick overview of what each chemical does, before we get to the numbers:

  • pH — Controls how acidic or basic the water is. Directly affects how well chlorine works. This is the most important number.
  • Total Alkalinity — The "shock absorber" for pH. Keeps pH from jumping around when chemicals are added.
  • Calcium Hardness — Measures dissolved calcium in the water. Too little and the water eats away at plaster surfaces. Too much and it leaves white scale buildup.
  • Free Chlorine (FC) — This is the amount of chlorine that's actually active and available to kill germs. The "free" part means it hasn't been used up yet.
  • CYA (Stabilizer) — Protects chlorine from being destroyed by the sun. We'll cover CYA in detail in the next section.

Now here are the target ranges. These are the numbers you need to memorize:

Parameter Ideal Range Too Low Too High
pH 7.2 – 7.6 Corrosive, eye irritation Kills chlorine, scaling
Total Alkalinity 80 – 120 ppm pH bounce, corrosion Cloudy water, scaling
Calcium Hardness 200 – 400 ppm Plaster etching Scaling on surfaces
Free Chlorine 1 – 3 ppm Algae, bacteria, illness Bleaches swimwear, irritates
CYA (Stabilizer) 30 – 50 ppm Chlorine burns off in sun Chlorine lock, ineffective FC
Memory trick: "pH is mood, alkalinity is the bodyguard." When alkalinity is right, pH stays stable. When alkalinity is low, pH bounces around wildly with every chemical addition — like a mood without a bodyguard.

3. CYA — Chlorine's Sunscreen

The problem: The sun destroys chlorine. UV rays from sunlight break down chlorine molecules and make them inactive. Without any protection, chlorine in an outdoor pool can disappear in just 2 to 4 hours of direct sun. That's expensive, and it leaves the pool unprotected.

The solution: CYA (short for cyanuric acid, also called a "stabilizer") acts like sunscreen for chlorine. It bonds loosely with chlorine molecules and shields them from UV rays, so they last much longer in the water. Most chlorine tablets already have CYA built into them.

The problem with too much CYA: Here's where it gets tricky. CYA builds up over time. Every time you add chlorine tablets, you add a little more CYA to the water. Unlike chlorine, CYA doesn't get used up — it just accumulates. When CYA gets too high (above 80–100 ppm), it grabs onto the chlorine so tightly that the chlorine can't do its job anymore. The pool test might show "2 ppm chlorine," but almost none of it is active. The pool can turn green even though the numbers look fine on paper. This is called over-stabilization.

The fix: There is no chemical that removes CYA. The only way to lower it is to drain some of the water out and refill with fresh water. This dilutes the CYA back to a safe level.
