1. The 8-Step Service Visit

Every service stop follows the same 8 steps — no exceptions, no shortcuts. The order isn't random. Each step sets up the next one. If you do them out of order, you create more work for yourself or actually make the pool worse.

For example: chemicals always go in last. Why? Because if you brush or vacuum after adding chemicals, you stir up debris that soaks up the chemicals unevenly — and you might scatter undissolved granules that bleach the surface. A clean, settled pool distributes chemicals evenly. Always clean first, then dose.

Why add chemicals LAST? Adding chemicals before or during cleaning stirs up debris that absorbs the chemicals unevenly. Brushing after shocking can scatter partially-dissolved shock and bleach the pool surface. A clean, settled pool distributes chemicals evenly — that's why cleaning always comes first.

2. Brushing Technique

What is biofilm? Biofilm is a thin, invisible layer of bacteria and organic material that clings to pool surfaces. You can't see it — but it's there. Over time, if it's not disturbed, it becomes the foundation for algae. By the time you can see green on the walls, the biofilm has already been growing for a while. Regular brushing breaks it up before it ever gets that far. That's why you brush every single visit, even when the pool looks perfectly clean.

Brushing is non-negotiable — even when the pool looks spotless. Brush every visit, every time.

Brush Selection by Surface Type

Plaster/Gunite
Combination nylon/stainless steel brush. The steel bristles scrub rough surfaces; nylon protects grout lines.
Vinyl Liner
Soft nylon bristles ONLY. Steel bristles will puncture or tear vinyl liner — a very expensive mistake.
Fiberglass
Soft nylon bristles. Fiberglass can scratch easily — never use stainless steel on fiberglass pools.

Brushing order: Start at the waterline and work down. Brush walls, then steps, then the floor edges. Use long, overlapping strokes. Push sediment toward the main drain.

Common beginner mistake: Skipping the brush because the pool "looks clean." Remember: the biofilm you're breaking up is invisible. Brushing once a week is what keeps it from ever becoming a visible problem.

3. Vacuuming Technique

After you brush, debris falls to the pool floor. Vacuuming picks it up. The key is patience — if you move the vacuum too fast, the suction can't grab the sediment. Instead, you'll stir it up into the water like a snow globe, and it'll just settle back down. Move slowly and let the vacuum do the work.

  • Move slowly — fast movement stirs sediment back into the water instead of picking it up
  • Overlap 25–30% on every pass — this ensures you don't miss any spots. Think of it like mowing a lawn with slight overlap on each row
  • Straight passes, one end to the other — systematic, not random zigzagging
  • Start in the deep end, work toward the shallow end — any sediment you disturb will drift toward shallow water, where you'll catch it on the next pass
  • Watch the pressure gauge — if pressure drops while vacuuming, your hose or filter is getting clogged. Stop and check it.
Common beginner mistake: Moving too quickly and stirring up sediment instead of picking it up. Slow and steady is always better. If the water clouds up, you're going too fast.
